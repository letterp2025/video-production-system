# Video Formats and Visual Treatments

A **format** describes how a video tells its story. A **visual treatment** describes how the video looks and moves. They are different choices and can be combined.

The examples below are starting points, not an exhaustive list or fixed taxonomy.

## Story formats

| Format | What it does | Useful when |
|---|---|---|
| Explainer | Moves from a question or problem to a clear explanation and takeaway. | The viewer needs to understand a concept, process, or decision. |
| Tutorial or product walkthrough | Shows a task and the steps or interface used to complete it. | The viewer needs practical instruction or proof of a workflow. |
| Reflective essay | Develops a human, cultural, or philosophical question through narration and examples. | The idea benefits from context and interpretation, not only a demonstration. |
| Case study | Follows a specific project, test, or outcome and explains what the evidence shows. | The viewer benefits from seeing decisions, results, and limitations. |

## Visual treatments

| Treatment | Typical qualities | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| High-kinetic product film | Fast, deliberate pacing, bold typography, close details, and tightly timed movement. | Energy can obscure the actual product or message. |
| Editorial illustration | Metaphors, characters, diagrams, or designed scenes support narration. | Metaphors need to stay clear and consistent. |
| Kinetic typography | Text size, layout, and movement carry the main visual rhythm. | Keep words readable long enough to understand. |
| Interface-led | Screen capture and restrained callouts show real software behavior. | Do not draw controls that are absent from the actual product. |
| Minimal motion graphics | Simple shapes, labels, and transitions clarify structure or data. | Decoration should not compete with the explanation. |

These qualities can mix within one video. An explainer can use interface capture for evidence, kinetic typography for the opening, and restrained diagrams for the explanation.

## Compare options before committing

Choose one idea and outline it in two or three format-treatment combinations. For each, note:

- The opening question or promise.
- The evidence or demonstration the viewer will see.
- The visual language and pace.
- What the viewer should remember.
- The main risk of confusion, overstatement, or visual overload.

This comparison is an exploration, not a script. Select a direction only after checking whether it fits the viewer and the available evidence. Then plan scenes with the [storyboard and visual planning guide](STORYBOARD_AND_VISUAL_PLANNING.md).
