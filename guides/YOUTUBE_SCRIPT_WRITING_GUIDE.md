# YouTube Script Writing Guide

This guide helps turn an outline, rough notes, or an early draft into spoken words that are clear, specific, and recognizably the creator's own. Adapt it to the creator's voice, the audience, and the format.

## Start with the creator's material

- Use the creator's outline, examples, observations, and chosen phrases as the starting point.
- Keep source wording visible while drafting. Distinguish it from suggested wording.
- An outline is a set of beats, not permission to flatten its personality or invent a new point of view.
- Treat capitalized notes, repetitions, and phrases such as "but then" or "the future" as possible performance or structure cues. Do not automatically copy a cue into the spoken text.
- Do not present invented opinions, personal experiences, or emotional reactions as the creator's.
- If a claim or transition is unclear, flag the gap instead of filling it with generic copy.

## Write for conversation

- Use ordinary spoken language, contractions, and sentence fragments when they sound natural.
- Vary sentence length and rhythm. Not every beat needs a neat final sentence.
- Keep a correction, repetition, question, or change of direction when it carries meaning.
- Preserve specific examples, uncertainty, and limitations. Do not make an unfinished test sound conclusive.
- Read the draft aloud. Revise lines that sound like an essay, an ad, or a template instead of a person speaking.
- Avoid imposing one punctuation rule or voice on every creator. Follow the project's chosen style while keeping the narration easy to say and understand.

## Avoid generic filler

- Do not add stock hooks, forced suspense, motivational lines, calls to action, or tidy summaries just because a template suggests them.
- Remove transitions that repeat what the viewer already knows.
- Keep an unusual phrase when it sounds natural and carries the creator's meaning.
- Replace vague statements with a concrete detail, example, demonstration, or supported claim.

## Work one beat at a time

1. Identify the viewer, the main promise, and the outline's intended beats.
2. Draft the requested beat without writing the rest of the script in advance when the creator wants to work incrementally.
3. Check names, numbers, product behavior, and test results against their sources. Mark estimates and open questions honestly.
4. Read the words aloud and revise for pacing, clarity, and the creator's natural phrasing.
5. Show the draft for review when the creator wants to read it before narration. Treat feedback as direction for the next pass.
6. Once a beat works in text, review a spoken recording or generated narration if available. Listening can reveal pauses, emphasis, pronunciation, and awkward joins that are hard to judge on the page.

## Use generated audio as a drafting aid

When the project uses generated narration, split a long script into natural beats and keep each take reviewable while editing. Listen to the individual take and the assembled read: a line can sound fine alone but abrupt next to the previous segment. Regenerate only the portions that need work, then listen to the full sequence again.

The [Audio Narration Generator](../tools/audio_narration_generator/README.md) uses an ordered JSON manifest as the source of truth for exact spoken text and playback order. It can reuse unchanged takes and regenerate segments whose text or generation settings changed. Keep voice recordings, transcripts, narration, and project-specific manifests with the project, not in this public repository.

## Review questions

- Does the opening make the reason to watch clear?
- Does the script preserve the creator's idea and specific details?
- Does the spoken version sound natural at normal speed?
- Did a revision remove a repetition or rough edge that carried meaning?
- Is every factual statement supported by what was observed or tested?
- Is there generic filler that can be removed?
- Can viewers follow the idea even when the delivery is informal?
